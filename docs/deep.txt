Artificial Intelligence (AI)|AI is the broad goal of making computers do things that normally need human thinking: understanding language, recognising images, making decisions, learning from experience. It is an umbrella term. Machine learning, deep learning and generative AI are all ways of achieving it, and most AI today is narrow, built for one kind of task.|Your phone unlocking when it recognises your face, a map app finding the fastest route, and a chatbot answering questions are all AI.
Machine Learning (ML)|Instead of a programmer writing every rule, a machine-learning system is shown many examples and adjusts itself to find the pattern. The more good examples it sees, the better it usually gets. It is the main way AI is built today.|A bank shows a model thousands of past transactions marked fraud or genuine; the model learns what fraud looks like and flags new suspicious payments.
Deep Learning|Deep learning is machine learning with neural networks that have many layers. Each layer learns slightly more abstract features, for example edges, then shapes, then whole objects. It needs lots of data and computing power, but it powers speech recognition, image recognition and modern chatbots.|A deep network learns to recognise cats in photos without anyone telling it what whiskers or ears are.
Neural Network|A neural network is a set of simple units (neurons) arranged in layers and connected by weighted links. Each neuron adds up its inputs, applies a simple function and passes the result on. Training adjusts the weights so the network's outputs match the examples. It is loosely inspired by the brain, but works very differently.|A small network takes the pixels of a handwritten digit as input and outputs which number from 0 to 9 it is.
Algorithm|An algorithm is a precise, step-by-step recipe a computer follows to solve a problem. In AI, there are algorithms for learning from data (like gradient descent) and algorithms for making decisions (like search). The algorithm is the method; the model is what the method produces.|A recipe for sorting a list of names alphabetically is an algorithm; so is the procedure a spam filter uses to learn from emails.
Model|A model is the result of training: a set of learned patterns, stored as numbers, that turns inputs into outputs. Once trained, you can save it, share it and use it to make predictions on new data. ChatGPT, a spam filter and a face-unlock system are each powered by a model.|After training on house sales, a price model takes a new house's size and location and outputs an estimated price.
Narrow AI (Weak AI)|Narrow AI is excellent at one specific task but cannot transfer that skill to unrelated tasks. Every AI system in use today is narrow, even impressive ones. A chess program cannot drive a car; a translation model cannot diagnose illness.|A spam filter is brilliant at spotting junk email but has no idea how to recommend a film.
Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)|AGI is a hypothetical AI that could learn and perform almost any intellectual task a human can, and transfer knowledge between areas. It does not exist yet, and experts disagree about when, or whether, it will. It is a major focus of AI research and safety debates.|An AGI could learn to cook, then write a novel, then do scientific research, without being built separately for each.
Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)|ASI describes an AI that would be far more capable than the best humans in every field, including science, strategy and social skills. It is purely theoretical. It matters mainly in discussions about the long-term risks and governance of AI.|Science fiction AIs that redesign themselves and outthink all of humanity are examples of imagined ASI.
Generative AI|Generative AI creates new content, such as text, images, music, video or code, by learning the patterns in huge amounts of existing content and producing something similar but new. It is different from AI that only classifies or predicts. Large language models and image generators are the best-known types.|Asking an AI to "draw a cat astronaut in watercolour" and getting a brand-new picture is generative AI.
Discriminative AI|Discriminative models learn to tell categories apart or predict a label for an input, rather than create new content. They answer questions like "which class is this?" Most traditional machine learning is discriminative.|A model that decides whether an X-ray shows pneumonia or not is discriminative.
Predictive AI|Predictive AI uses patterns in historical data to forecast what is likely to happen next. Businesses use it to plan stock, prices, staffing and risk. Its predictions are probabilities, not certainties, and they get worse if the future stops looking like the past.|A supermarket predicts how much ice cream it will sell next week from past sales and the weather forecast.
Conversational AI|Conversational AI lets people interact with computers through natural back-and-forth dialogue, in text or speech. It combines understanding what you mean, keeping track of the conversation and generating a sensible reply. Modern versions are usually built on large language models.|A bank's chat assistant that understands "I lost my card, what do I do?" and walks you through blocking it.
Chatbot|A chatbot is any program you talk to in messages. Older chatbots followed fixed scripts and keyword rules; modern ones use large language models to understand and generate free-form replies. Their quality depends on the model behind them and the instructions they are given.|A shopping website's help bubble that answers "Where is my order?" is a chatbot.
Data Science|Data science combines statistics, programming and subject knowledge to turn raw data into insight and decisions. It includes collecting and cleaning data, exploring it, building models and explaining the results. Machine learning is one of its main tools.|A data scientist analyses customer data to find out why people cancel subscriptions and which ones are likely to cancel next.
Cognitive Computing|Cognitive computing was a popular term, especially at IBM, for systems that aim to mimic human thinking: understanding language, reasoning over evidence and learning from interactions. Today the same ideas are usually just called AI.|IBM Watson reading thousands of medical papers to suggest possible treatments was marketed as cognitive computing.
Expert System|An expert system captures human experts' knowledge as a large set of if-then rules, then applies them to new cases. They were the leading form of AI in the 1970s and 80s. They work well in narrow areas but are hard to build and maintain, and they cannot learn on their own.|MYCIN, an early medical expert system, used about 600 rules to suggest antibiotics for infections.
Symbolic AI|Symbolic AI represents knowledge with human-readable symbols and logical rules, then reasons with them. It is transparent and precise but brittle when the world is messy. Modern AI is mostly statistical (learned from data), though researchers increasingly combine both approaches.|A program that knows "all birds have wings" and "a robin is a bird" and concludes "a robin has wings".
Rule-based System|A rule-based system makes decisions using rules that people wrote by hand, such as "if the amount is over $10,000, flag for review". It is predictable and easy to explain, but every situation must be anticipated in advance. Many real products mix rules with machine learning.|A loan form that automatically rejects applicants under 18 is following a simple rule.
Heuristic|A heuristic is a practical shortcut or rule of thumb that usually gives a good-enough answer quickly, without guaranteeing the best one. AI uses heuristics to avoid checking every possibility, which would often take far too long.|A route planner preferring roads that head roughly towards the destination is using a heuristic.
Input / Output|Every AI system takes something in and gives something out. The input might be text, an image, numbers or sound; the output might be a label, a number, an answer or new content. Defining these clearly is the first step in designing any AI system.|For a translation app, the input is an English sentence and the output is the French sentence.
Prediction|A prediction is the model's output for an input it has not seen before. It may be a category, a number or a probability. Predictions come with uncertainty, so good systems often report how confident they are.|A weather model predicting a 70% chance of rain tomorrow.
Pattern Recognition|Pattern recognition is the ability to spot regularities in data and use them to classify or predict. It is at the heart of almost all machine learning, from recognising faces to detecting fraud. The patterns can be visual, sound-based, numerical or textual.|A music app recognising a song from a few seconds of audio by matching its sound pattern.
Automation|Automation means using technology to do tasks without human effort. Traditional automation follows fixed steps; AI-powered automation can handle tasks that involve judgement, language or images. It can save time and reduce errors, but it also changes jobs.|An accounts system that automatically reads invoices, extracts amounts and pays approved bills.
Intelligent Agent|An intelligent agent is anything that senses its environment, decides what to do and acts to achieve a goal. The idea covers simple systems like thermostats as well as advanced AI. Today's AI agents use language models to plan and use tools.|A robot vacuum that senses walls, decides where to go next and cleans the room is an intelligent agent.
Data|Data is the raw material of AI: numbers, text, images, audio, clicks and sensor readings. Models learn only what is in their data, so its quantity, quality and fairness largely decide how good the AI will be.|Millions of labelled photos are the data an image-recognition model learns from.
Dataset|A dataset is a structured collection of examples gathered for a purpose, usually stored as tables, files or folders. Good datasets are large enough, accurate, varied and representative of the real situations where the model will be used. Famous datasets are often used as shared benchmarks.|ImageNet, with over 14 million labelled images, is one of the most famous datasets in AI history.
Training Data|Training data is the portion of the data the model actually learns from. Its patterns, and its mistakes and biases, are what the model absorbs. If the training data doesn't include a situation, the model will probably handle it poorly.|A spam filter's training data is thousands of emails already marked spam or not spam.
Validation Data|Validation data is held back from training and used while building the model to compare versions and tune settings, such as how complex the model should be. It gives an honest check during development without touching the final test set.|You try three model sizes and keep the one that scores best on the validation data.
Test Data|Test data is kept completely aside until the very end, then used once to estimate how the model will perform on genuinely new data. If you peek at it or tune against it, the score becomes over-optimistic.|After all tuning is finished, you run the spam filter on 2,000 unseen emails to report its final accuracy.
Train/Test Split|Splitting the data, for example 80% for training and 20% for testing, lets you measure how well a model generalises rather than how well it memorised. The split should be random and should keep similar examples, such as photos of the same person, on one side only.|From 10,000 labelled emails, 8,000 are used for training and 2,000 are kept for testing.
Label|A label is the correct answer attached to an example. In supervised learning, labels are what the model is trying to learn to predict. Labels are often created by people, which makes them expensive and sometimes inconsistent.|In a dataset of animal photos, the label of each photo is the animal's name, such as "cat" or "dog".
Labeled Data|Labeled data comes with correct answers attached and is needed for supervised learning. It is valuable because labelling often takes human time and expertise, especially in fields like medicine or law.|Thousands of X-rays each marked "fracture" or "no fracture" by radiologists.
Unlabeled Data|Unlabeled data has no answers attached. It is cheap and plentiful, for example all the text on the internet. It can be used for unsupervised learning, or for self-supervised learning where the data provides its own training signal.|Millions of customer reviews with no star ratings or categories attached.
Annotation|Annotation is the work of adding labels or extra information to data, such as drawing boxes around objects or marking the sentiment of a sentence. It is often done by teams of people with clear guidelines, and its quality directly affects the model.|Workers drawing boxes around every pedestrian in street photos so a self-driving car can learn to detect people.
Ground Truth|Ground truth is the verified, real answer you use to judge whether a model is right. If the ground truth itself is wrong, your evaluation will be misleading too.|Biopsy results are the ground truth for checking a model that predicts cancer from scans.
Feature|A feature is one measurable property of the input that the model uses to make its prediction. Choosing informative features is a big part of classic machine learning; deep learning often learns its own features from raw data.|For predicting house prices, features include floor area, number of bedrooms, age and location.
Feature Engineering|Feature engineering means creating new, more useful inputs from raw data using knowledge of the problem. Good features can improve a model more than switching to a fancier algorithm.|From a purchase date you create "day of the week" and "is it a holiday?" features to predict shop sales.
Feature Selection|Feature selection keeps only the inputs that genuinely help, and removes those that are irrelevant or redundant. It makes models simpler, faster, easier to explain and less likely to overfit.|Dropping "customer ID number" from a model, because it carries no real meaning and only adds noise.
Target Variable|The target variable is the thing you want the model to predict, also called the output or label. Choosing the right target is a key design decision, because the model will optimise exactly that and nothing else.|In a model predicting whether a customer will cancel next month, the target is "cancelled: yes or no".
Structured Data|Structured data is organised in rows and columns with a fixed format, like a spreadsheet or database table. It is easy to search and analyse, and classic machine learning works very well on it.|A table of customers with columns for age, city, signup date and monthly spend.
Unstructured Data|Unstructured data has no fixed table format: text, images, audio, video and documents. Most of the world's data is unstructured. Deep learning made it possible to learn from it directly.|Emails, product photos and recorded customer calls are unstructured data.
Semi-structured Data|Semi-structured data has some organisation, such as labels or tags, but not a strict table. It sits between structured and unstructured data and is very common in web systems.|A JSON file describing a product, with fields like name and price but optional extra fields.
Big Data|Big data refers to datasets so large, fast-moving or varied that ordinary tools can't handle them. It needs special storage and processing systems. Large AI models are trained on big data.|The billions of searches, clicks and videos generated online every day are big data.
Data Cleaning|Data cleaning fixes or removes errors such as typos, duplicates, missing values and impossible entries before training. It often takes more time than building the model, and it is one of the most effective ways to improve results.|Removing duplicate customer records and correcting ages entered as 250 instead of 25.
Data Preprocessing|Preprocessing transforms cleaned data into the exact form a model needs: scaling numbers, encoding categories, splitting text into tokens or resizing images. The same steps must be applied to new data when the model is used.|Resizing every photo to 224 by 224 pixels and scaling pixel values to between 0 and 1.
Data Wrangling|Data wrangling, or munging, is the hands-on work of reshaping messy real-world data into a usable form: merging sources, fixing formats and restructuring tables. It is a large part of everyday data work.|Combining sales data from three shops whose spreadsheets use different date formats and product names.
Normalization|Normalization rescales numbers to a common range, often 0 to 1, so that features measured in different units can be compared fairly. Without it, a feature with large numbers can dominate one with small numbers.|Rescaling incomes (0 to 200,000) and ages (0 to 100) both to 0 to 1 before training.
Standardization|Standardization rescales a feature so that its average becomes 0 and its typical spread (standard deviation) becomes 1. It is a common alternative to normalization and helps many algorithms train faster and more evenly.|Converting exam scores so that an average score becomes 0 and a score one standard deviation above becomes 1.
Missing Values|Missing values are empty entries in a dataset, such as unanswered survey questions or failed sensor readings. Many algorithms can't handle them, so you remove those rows, fill them in, or let the model treat "missing" as information.|Some customers left the "income" field blank on a signup form.
Imputation|Imputation fills in missing values with sensible estimates, such as the average, the most common value, or a prediction from other features. It keeps data usable, but bad imputation can hide or invent patterns.|Filling a missing age with the median age of similar customers.
Outlier|An outlier is a value far from the rest of the data. It might be an error, which you should fix, or a genuinely rare and important case, which you might want to study. Outliers can distort averages and some models.|A house listed at $1 in a dataset where prices range from $100,000 to $900,000.
Noise|Noise is random variation or errors in data that don't reflect the real pattern, such as measurement mistakes or mislabelled examples. A good model learns the pattern and ignores the noise; an overfit model learns the noise too.|A temperature sensor whose readings jump randomly by a degree or two.
Data Augmentation|Data augmentation creates extra training examples by making small, realistic changes to existing ones. It helps models handle variety and reduces overfitting, especially when data is limited.|Flipping, rotating, cropping and brightening photos so an image model sees each picture in many forms.
Synthetic Data|Synthetic data is artificially generated to look like real data. It is useful when real data is scarce, expensive or private, but it can miss the messiness of reality and copy the biases of whatever generated it.|Generating realistic fake patient records to train a hospital model without exposing real patients' details.
Data Pipeline|A data pipeline is an automated chain of steps that collects data, cleans and transforms it, and delivers it where it is needed, such as to a model for training or predictions. Reliable pipelines keep AI systems fed with fresh, consistent data.|Every night a pipeline pulls the day's sales, cleans them and updates the demand-forecasting model.
ETL (Extract, Transform, Load)|ETL is a classic pipeline pattern: extract data from its sources, transform it into a clean, consistent format, and load it into a store for analysis. It is the backbone of most business reporting and many AI systems.|Extracting orders from an online shop, converting currencies, and loading them into a sales database.
Data Lake|A data lake stores huge amounts of raw data in its original form, structured or not, until someone needs it. It is cheap and flexible, but without good organisation it can become a messy "data swamp".|A company dumps all its logs, images and spreadsheets into cloud storage for future analysis.
Data Warehouse|A data warehouse stores cleaned, structured data that is organised for fast analysis and reporting. It is more curated than a data lake and is used for dashboards and business intelligence.|A retailer's warehouse holds tidy tables of sales by product, store and day, ready for managers' reports.
Data Mining|Data mining searches large datasets for useful patterns, relationships and anomalies. It overlaps heavily with machine learning and statistics.|Discovering that customers who buy baby products often also buy coffee.
Data Labeling|Data labeling is the process, often a whole industry, of having people or tools attach correct answers to raw data. Clear instructions, quality checks and fair working conditions all matter.|Thousands of workers tagging the sentiment of social media posts as positive, negative or neutral.
Imbalanced Data|Data is imbalanced when one class is much rarer than another. Models then tend to favour the common class, and plain accuracy becomes misleading. Fixes include collecting more rare examples, resampling or weighting, and using better measures.|Only 1 in 1,000 card transactions is fraud, so a model that always says "not fraud" is 99.9% accurate but useless.
Sampling|Sampling chooses a subset of data to study or train on. A good sample represents the whole population; a biased sample leads to biased conclusions and biased models.|Surveying 1,000 randomly selected voters to estimate how a whole country will vote.
One-hot Encoding|One-hot encoding turns a category into a set of yes/no columns, one per possible value, so a model can use it as numbers without implying a false order between categories.|"Colour = red, green or blue" becomes three columns: is_red, is_green, is_blue.
Dimensionality|Dimensionality is the number of features describing each example. Very high dimensionality makes data sparse and models harder to train, a problem often called "the curse of dimensionality".|A 100 by 100 pixel greyscale image has 10,000 dimensions, one per pixel.
Data Leakage|Data leakage happens when information that won't be available at prediction time sneaks into training. Test scores look brilliant, but the model fails in real use. It is one of the most common and costly mistakes in machine learning.|Predicting whether a patient has a disease using a feature that records the treatment for that disease.
Corpus|A corpus is a large, organised collection of text used to study language or train language models. Its size, languages, topics and quality shape what a model knows and how it writes.|Large language models are trained on corpora of trillions of words drawn from books, websites and code.
Web Scraping|Web scraping uses programs to automatically collect data from websites. It is a major source of training data, but it raises legal, ethical and copyright questions, and websites may forbid it.|A script that collects product prices from online shops every day to track price changes.
Metadata|Metadata is data about data: when a file was created, who made it, its size, location or format. It helps organise data, track where it came from and judge whether it can be trusted.|A photo's metadata records the camera model, the date, and sometimes the GPS location where it was taken.
Data Drift|Data drift happens when the data a model receives in real use slowly changes compared with its training data, so its accuracy quietly drops. It is detected by monitoring and fixed by retraining on recent data.|A shopping model trained before a pandemic suddenly sees very different buying habits.
Supervised Learning|In supervised learning the model learns from examples that come with the right answer. It compares its guesses with the answers, adjusts itself, and repeats until its guesses are good. It is the most common type of machine learning in business.|Learning to predict house prices from thousands of past sales where the selling price is known.
Unsupervised Learning|Unsupervised learning finds structure in data without any answers given. It groups similar items, spots unusual ones or simplifies the data. A person then interprets what the groups mean.|A streaming service discovering natural groups of viewers with similar tastes, without defining the groups in advance.
Semi-supervised Learning|Semi-supervised learning combines a small amount of labelled data with a large amount of unlabelled data. The labelled examples guide the model, and the unlabelled ones help it understand the overall shape of the data. It is useful when labelling is expensive.|Training a medical image model with 500 images labelled by doctors plus 50,000 unlabelled images.
Self-supervised Learning|In self-supervised learning the data creates its own labels, for example by hiding part of the input and asking the model to predict it. This lets models learn from enormous unlabelled datasets. It is how large language models are pre-trained.|Hiding the next word of a sentence and training the model to guess it, billions of times.
Reinforcement Learning (RL)|In reinforcement learning an agent learns by acting in an environment and receiving rewards or penalties. Over many attempts it discovers which actions lead to the most reward. It suits problems with sequences of decisions, like games and robotics.|An AI learning to play a video game by trial and error, scoring points as its reward.
Transfer Learning|Transfer learning reuses a model trained on one task as the starting point for another. Because it already knows general patterns, it needs far less data and time to learn the new task. It is why small teams can build strong models.|Taking a model trained on millions of everyday photos and fine-tuning it on 1,000 images to spot crop diseases.
Online Learning|Online learning updates a model continuously as each new piece of data arrives, instead of retraining from scratch. It suits fast-changing situations, but it needs safeguards so bad data doesn't corrupt the model.|A news recommender that adjusts to what readers click on throughout the day.
Batch Learning|Batch learning trains a model on a whole fixed dataset at once, then uses it unchanged until the next retraining. It is simpler and more stable than online learning, but slower to react to change.|Retraining a fraud model every Sunday night on all of the past year's transactions.
Active Learning|In active learning the model picks the examples it is most unsure about and asks a person to label just those. This gets the most improvement from the fewest labels, saving expensive expert time.|A legal-document classifier asks a lawyer to label only the 50 contracts it finds most confusing.
Federated Learning|Federated learning trains a shared model across many devices or organisations without collecting their raw data in one place. Each device learns locally and sends only model updates, which helps protect privacy.|Phone keyboards improve word suggestions by learning on each phone without uploading what people type.
Multi-task Learning|Multi-task learning trains one model on several related tasks at once. Lessons from one task can help the others, and one model is cheaper to run than several.|A single model for self-driving cars that detects lanes, pedestrians and traffic signs at the same time.
Meta-learning|Meta-learning, or "learning to learn", trains models on many different tasks so they can adapt to a brand-new task from just a few examples. It is about learning good ways to learn, not just one skill.|A model trained on hundreds of small classification tasks can learn a new type of flower from five photos.
Zero-shot Learning|Zero-shot learning means handling a task or category the model was never specifically trained on, by using general knowledge or a description. Large language models do this often.|Asking a chatbot to classify reviews as "about delivery" or "about quality" without giving it any examples.
One-shot Learning|One-shot learning means learning a new category from a single example. Humans do this naturally; models usually need help from prior training to manage it.|A face-recognition door system that learns a new employee's face from one photo.
Few-shot Learning|Few-shot learning means learning a task from only a handful of examples. With large language models, you can often do it just by putting the examples in the prompt.|Showing a chatbot three examples of turning a product feature into a benefit, then asking for a fourth.
Contrastive Learning|Contrastive learning teaches a model by pulling similar pairs together and pushing different pairs apart in its internal representation. It is powerful for learning from unlabelled data and for linking images with text.|Training on photos with their captions so that a picture of a dog ends up close to the words "a dog".
Curriculum Learning|Curriculum learning trains a model on easy examples first and gradually introduces harder ones, much like a school curriculum. It can make training faster and more stable.|Teaching a model to read short, clear sentences before long, complicated ones.
Ensemble Learning|Ensemble learning combines several models and merges their predictions, by voting or averaging. Different models make different mistakes, so the combination is usually more accurate and reliable than any one of them.|A random forest averages hundreds of decision trees to predict loan risk.
Classification|Classification predicts which category an input belongs to. The output is a label, often with a probability for each possible class. It is one of the most common machine-learning tasks.|Sorting emails into spam or not spam, or photos into cat, dog or bird.
Binary Classification|Binary classification has exactly two possible outcomes, such as yes or no. Its quality is usually judged with precision, recall and the confusion matrix, not just accuracy.|Predicting whether a patient does or does not have a disease.
Multi-class Classification|Multi-class classification picks exactly one label from three or more options. The model outputs a probability for each class and chooses the most likely one.|Recognising which of the 10 digits, 0 to 9, a handwritten number is.
Multi-label Classification|In multi-label classification each item can belong to several categories at once, so the model makes a separate yes/no decision for each label.|Tagging a photo as "beach", "sunset" and "people" all at the same time.
Regression|Regression predicts a continuous number rather than a category. It is judged by how far its predictions are from the true values, using measures like mean absolute error.|Predicting tomorrow's temperature, a house's price or how long a delivery will take.
Clustering|Clustering groups similar items together without labels. The algorithm decides which items are similar, usually by distance between their features, and people interpret what each group means.|Grouping shoppers into "bargain hunters", "brand loyalists" and "occasional buyers" based on purchase history.
Anomaly Detection|Anomaly detection learns what "normal" looks like and flags things that don't fit. It is used where unusual events are rare but important, often without many labelled examples of the anomalies.|A bank flagging a card that suddenly makes ten large purchases abroad in an hour.
Dimensionality Reduction|Dimensionality reduction compresses data with many features into fewer while keeping the important information. It speeds up models, reduces noise and makes it possible to plot complex data in 2D.|Reducing 1,000 survey answers per person to three summary scores that capture most of the differences.
Association Rules|Association rules find items or events that often occur together and express them as "if X then Y" rules with a measure of strength. They are widely used for product recommendations and store layouts.|"Customers who buy pasta and tomato sauce also buy parmesan 40% of the time."
Recommendation System|A recommendation system predicts what each user is likely to want, based on their behaviour, what similar users liked, and the items' features. It drives much of what we see on shopping, video and music sites.|Netflix suggesting a series because people with similar viewing histories watched it.
Time Series Forecasting|Time series forecasting predicts future values from data recorded over time, taking account of trends, seasons and cycles. Unexpected events can break forecasts, so they come with uncertainty.|Forecasting electricity demand for each hour of next week from past usage and weather.
Ranking|Ranking orders a list of items by relevance or preference rather than labelling them one by one. Search engines, feeds and recommendation lists all depend on ranking models.|A search engine ordering millions of web pages so the most useful appear first.
Training|Training is the process of showing a model examples and repeatedly adjusting its parameters to reduce its errors. It can take seconds for a small model or months on thousands of chips for a large language model.|A spam filter is trained by processing thousands of labelled emails and adjusting itself after each batch.
Parameters|Parameters are the internal numbers a model learns during training, such as weights and biases. They store everything the model has learned. Large language models have billions of them.|A simple line-fitting model has two parameters, slope and intercept; GPT-scale models have hundreds of billions.
Weights|Weights are parameters that control how strongly each input or connection influences the output. Training is mostly the process of adjusting weights. A large weight means "this matters a lot".|In a house-price model, a large weight on "floor area" means size strongly affects the predicted price.
Bias (parameter)|A bias parameter is a learned constant added to a neuron's calculation, letting it shift its output up or down independently of the inputs. It is a different thing from unfair "AI bias".|The intercept in a line-fitting model, the predicted value when all inputs are zero, acts as a bias.
Hyperparameters|Hyperparameters are settings chosen before training that control how the model learns, rather than what it learns. Choosing them well can make a big difference to the final result.|The learning rate, the number of layers and the batch size are all hyperparameters.
Hyperparameter Tuning|Hyperparameter tuning searches for the best settings by training many versions and comparing them on validation data. It can be done by hand, by grid or random search, or by smarter automated methods.|Trying learning rates of 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 and keeping the one with the lowest validation error.
Grid Search|Grid search tries every combination of a chosen set of hyperparameter values. It is simple and thorough but becomes very slow as more settings are added.|Testing 3 learning rates times 4 depths, which is 12 models, and picking the best.
Random Search|Random search tries random combinations of hyperparameters within chosen ranges. It often finds good settings faster than grid search because not all settings matter equally.|Sampling 20 random combinations of learning rate and depth instead of testing all 100.
Learning Rate|The learning rate controls how big a step the model takes each time it adjusts its weights. Too large and training overshoots and becomes unstable; too small and it learns painfully slowly.|Like adjusting a shower temperature: big turns overshoot, tiny turns take forever.
Epoch|An epoch is one complete pass through all the training data. Models are usually trained for several or many epochs, and validation error is checked after each to decide when to stop.|With 10,000 images and a batch size of 100, one epoch is 100 training steps.
Batch|A batch is a small group of examples processed together before the weights are updated. Batches make training faster on GPUs and smoother than updating after every single example.|Processing 32 images at a time, averaging their errors, then adjusting the weights once.
Batch Size|Batch size is the number of examples in each batch. Larger batches are faster per example and give steadier updates but need more memory; smaller batches are noisier but can generalise well.|A batch size of 64 means 64 examples are processed before each update.
Iteration (Step)|An iteration or step is one update of the model's weights, after processing one batch. Training progress is often measured in steps.|Training for 50,000 steps means the weights were adjusted 50,000 times.
Loss Function|The loss function turns "how wrong was the model?" into a single number for each prediction. Training tries to make this number as small as possible, so choosing the right loss defines what "good" means.|For price predictions, the loss might be the squared difference between predicted and actual price.
Cost Function|The cost function is usually the average loss over all training examples, sometimes plus penalties for complexity. In practice people often use "cost" and "loss" to mean the same thing.|The average squared error across all 10,000 houses in the training set.
Objective Function|The objective function is whatever quantity training is trying to minimise or maximise. It may be a loss to minimise or a reward to maximise. The model will chase exactly this, so it must match what you really want.|In reinforcement learning, the objective is to maximise total reward.
Gradient|The gradient tells you, for every weight, which direction to change it and how sensitive the error is to that weight. It is calculated with calculus and is the key ingredient of training.|Like feeling which way the ground slopes under your feet when walking downhill in fog.
Gradient Descent|Gradient descent repeatedly nudges every weight a little in the direction that lowers the loss most steeply. Step by step, the model walks downhill towards lower error. It is the core training method of modern AI.|Training a network by taking millions of small downhill steps on its error.
Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)|SGD estimates the gradient from a small random batch instead of the whole dataset, making each step much faster. The noise in these estimates can even help the model find better solutions.|Updating the model after each batch of 32 random examples rather than after all one million.
Optimizer|The optimizer is the algorithm that uses gradients to update the weights. Different optimizers add tricks like momentum or adaptive step sizes to train faster and more reliably.|SGD, Adam and RMSprop are common optimizers.
Adam|Adam is a popular optimizer that gives each weight its own adaptive step size and uses momentum to smooth updates. It works well with little tuning, so it is the default for many deep learning projects.|Most transformer language models are trained with Adam or a close variant called AdamW.
Convergence|A model has converged when further training no longer meaningfully improves the loss. Reaching convergence doesn't guarantee the model is good; it may have settled on a mediocre solution.|After 30 epochs the validation loss stops dropping, so training has converged.
Local Minimum|A local minimum is a low point in the error that isn't the lowest possible. Training can get stuck in it. In very large networks, many such points turn out to be almost as good as the best.|Like a hiker reaching the bottom of a small valley without seeing the deeper valley over the next hill.
Overfitting|Overfitting is when a model learns the training data too closely, including its noise and quirks, so it performs very well on training data but poorly on new data. It is like memorising answers instead of understanding.|A model scores 99% on its training data but only 65% on new examples.
Underfitting|Underfitting is when a model is too simple to capture the real pattern, so it performs poorly on both training and new data. The fix is usually a more capable model or better features.|Trying to predict house prices from a single feature with a straight line, missing most of what drives price.
Generalization|Generalization is a model's ability to perform well on new, unseen data. It is the whole point of machine learning: a model that only works on its training examples is useless.|A face-unlock model that still recognises you with new glasses or in different lighting generalises well.
Bias-Variance Tradeoff|The bias-variance trade-off describes the tension between models that are too simple, with high bias, and models that are too sensitive to their training data, with high variance. Good models find a balance that generalises best.|A straight line underfits a curved pattern (high bias); a wildly wiggly curve overfits it (high variance).
Regularization|Regularization adds techniques or penalties that discourage overly complex models, nudging them towards simpler patterns that generalise better. It is a standard defence against overfitting.|Adding a penalty for large weights so the model can't rely too heavily on any one feature.
L1 / L2 Regularization|L1 and L2 regularization add a penalty based on the size of the weights. L2 shrinks all weights smoothly; L1 can push some weights to exactly zero, effectively removing unimportant features.|With L1, a model predicting sales ends up ignoring 30 of its 50 features entirely.
Early Stopping|Early stopping halts training when the error on validation data stops improving, even if training error is still falling. This catches the moment overfitting begins and also saves time.|Training stops at epoch 12 because validation error has risen for three epochs in a row.
Cross-validation|Cross-validation tests a model on several different held-out parts of the data and averages the results. This gives a more reliable estimate of performance than a single split, especially with small datasets.|Training and testing five times on different splits, then reporting the average accuracy.
K-fold Cross-validation|K-fold cross-validation splits the data into k equal parts, trains on k minus 1 parts and tests on the remaining one, rotating until every part has been the test set once.|With 5-fold cross-validation on 1,000 examples, each round tests on 200 and trains on 800.
Checkpoint|A checkpoint is a saved snapshot of a model's weights during training. Checkpoints let you resume after a crash, compare versions, or roll back to an earlier, better version.|Saving the model every 1,000 steps so a power cut doesn't lose a week of training.
Pre-training|Pre-training is the first, large-scale training stage on broad, general data. It gives a model wide knowledge and skills that can later be specialised. It is the most expensive part of building a large model.|A language model reading trillions of words from the web before it learns to be a helpful assistant.
Fine-tuning|Fine-tuning continues training a pre-trained model on a smaller, focused dataset so it specialises in a task, a style or a domain. It is far cheaper than training from scratch.|Fine-tuning a general language model on a company's support tickets so it answers in the company's style.
Warm-up|Warm-up starts training with a very small learning rate and gradually raises it over the first steps. This prevents large, unstable updates while the model's weights are still random.|Increasing the learning rate from almost zero to its full value over the first 2,000 steps.
Learning Rate Schedule|A learning rate schedule changes the learning rate during training, usually starting higher for fast progress and lowering it later for fine adjustment.|Halving the learning rate every 10 epochs so the model settles precisely into a good solution.
Vanishing Gradient|Vanishing gradients happen when the training signal shrinks as it travels back through many layers, so the early layers barely learn. Techniques like ReLU activations and residual connections help prevent it.|Very deep networks from the 1990s often failed to train because their first layers hardly changed.
Exploding Gradient|Exploding gradients happen when the training signal grows huge as it passes back through layers, causing wild weight updates and broken training. Gradient clipping and careful initialisation help control it.|The loss suddenly shoots up to infinity in the middle of training.
Gradient Clipping|Gradient clipping caps the size of gradients before each update so no single step can be dangerously large. It is a simple safeguard against exploding gradients.|Limiting the overall gradient size to 1.0 when training a recurrent network.
Catastrophic Forgetting|Catastrophic forgetting is when a model trained on new data loses skills it learned earlier. It is a key challenge for models that need to keep learning over time.|A model fine-tuned only on medical text becomes noticeably worse at everyday conversation.
Training Run|A training run is one complete session of training a model with specific data, settings and code. Large runs are carefully planned and monitored because they are so expensive.|A frontier language model's training run can take months and cost tens of millions of dollars.
Compute Budget|The compute budget is how much processing power and time you can afford for training. It strongly limits how large a model can be and how much data it can learn from.|A university team with a few GPUs must train a far smaller model than a big tech lab.
Neuron (Node)|An artificial neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up with a bias, and passes the total through an activation function to produce its output. On its own it is very simple; the power comes from millions of them connected in layers.|A neuron might take pixel brightness values as inputs and fire strongly when it sees a vertical edge.
Perceptron|The perceptron, invented in 1958, is the simplest neural network: a single neuron that draws a straight line to separate two classes. It can't learn patterns that aren't separable by a straight line, which led to multi-layer networks.|A perceptron can learn to separate "pass" from "fail" using study hours and attendance.
Layer|A layer is a group of neurons that all process the same input at the same stage. Data flows through layers one after another, with each layer building more abstract features from the previous one.|In an image network, early layers detect edges, middle layers detect shapes, and late layers detect objects.
Input Layer|The input layer receives the raw data and passes it into the network. It has one input for each feature, such as each pixel of an image or each number in a table row.|For a 28 by 28 pixel image, the input layer has 784 inputs.
Hidden Layer|Hidden layers sit between the input and output layers and do most of the learning. They are "hidden" because their values aren't directly seen, only used inside. More hidden layers make a network "deep".|A network with five hidden layers can learn more complex patterns than one with a single hidden layer.
Output Layer|The output layer produces the final result. Its shape matches the task: one number for regression, or one value per class, often turned into probabilities, for classification.|For digit recognition, the output layer has 10 neurons, one for each digit from 0 to 9.
Activation Function|An activation function decides each neuron's output from its weighted input, adding non-linearity. Without it, even a deep network could only learn straight-line relationships.|ReLU passes positive values through and turns negative values into zero.
ReLU|ReLU (rectified linear unit) outputs the input if it is positive and zero otherwise. It is simple, fast and helps deep networks train well, so it is one of the most widely used activations.|ReLU turns 3.2 into 3.2 and −1.5 into 0.
Sigmoid|The sigmoid function squashes any number into the range 0 to 1, producing an S-shaped curve. It is handy for probabilities in yes/no outputs, but it can slow learning in deep hidden layers.|A sigmoid output of 0.92 can be read as a 92% chance an email is spam.
Tanh|Tanh squashes values into the range −1 to 1 and is centred on zero, which often helps learning compared with sigmoid. It was common in older recurrent networks.|Tanh turns a large positive input into nearly 1 and a large negative input into nearly −1.
Softmax|Softmax turns a list of raw scores into probabilities that are all positive and add up to 1. It is used in the output layer for choosing between several classes, and to pick the next word in language models.|Scores of 2, 1 and 0.1 for cat, dog and bird become probabilities of about 66%, 24% and 10%.
Forward Propagation|Forward propagation is passing an input through the network layer by layer to compute the output. It happens every time the model makes a prediction, during training and in use.|An image goes in at the input layer and a "cat: 91%" prediction comes out of the output layer.
Backpropagation|Backpropagation works out how much each weight contributed to the error by passing the error backwards through the network with calculus. Those values (the gradients) are then used to update the weights. It is the engine of neural network training.|After predicting "dog" for a cat photo, backpropagation finds which weights to adjust, and by how much, to make "cat" more likely.
Feedforward Network|In a feedforward network, information moves in one direction only, from input to output, with no loops. It is the simplest kind of neural network and works well for fixed-size inputs like table rows.|A network that predicts loan default from 20 applicant features, layer by layer.
Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)|An MLP is a feedforward network with one or more hidden layers of fully connected neurons. With non-linear activations it can learn very complex patterns, and MLP blocks also appear inside transformers.|A three-layer MLP that classifies handwritten digits.
Fully Connected (Dense) Layer|In a fully connected layer every neuron receives input from every neuron in the previous layer. It is flexible but uses many parameters, so it is often used near the end of a network.|The final layers of an image classifier that combine all detected features into a decision.
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)|A CNN scans images with small learnable filters that detect local patterns wherever they appear, then combines them into larger patterns. Sharing filters across the image makes CNNs efficient and good at vision.|CNNs power photo tagging, medical image analysis and the camera systems in self-driving cars.
Convolution|A convolution slides a small grid of weights (a filter) across the input, computing a weighted sum at each position. The result shows where a particular pattern appears.|A 3 by 3 filter that lights up wherever there is a horizontal edge in a photo.
Kernel (Filter)|A kernel or filter is the small grid of weights used in a convolution. Each filter learns to detect one kind of pattern, and a layer usually has dozens or hundreds of them.|One filter learns to detect diagonal lines, another learns a patch of fur texture.
Pooling|Pooling shrinks a feature map by summarising small regions, often by taking the largest value. It reduces computation and makes the network less sensitive to exactly where a pattern appears.|Max pooling turns each 2 by 2 block of values into its single largest value, halving the width and height.
Feature Map|A feature map is the output of applying one filter across an image: a grid showing how strongly that pattern appears at each location. Deeper feature maps represent more complex patterns.|A feature map that is bright wherever the image contains an eye.
Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)|An RNN processes sequences one step at a time, carrying a hidden "memory" forward from each step to the next. It was the standard for language and speech before transformers, but struggles with long sequences.|An RNN reading a sentence word by word to predict whether a review is positive.
LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)|An LSTM is a type of RNN with "gates" that decide what to remember, what to forget and what to output. This lets it keep information over much longer sequences than a basic RNN.|LSTMs powered Google Translate and phone speech recognition in the mid-2010s.
GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit)|A GRU is a simplified version of an LSTM with fewer gates. It is faster to train and often performs about as well on many sequence tasks.|A GRU forecasting energy demand from the last 48 hours of readings.
Transformer|The transformer, introduced in 2017, processes all the words in a sequence at once and uses attention to connect related words, however far apart they are. It trains efficiently on huge datasets and is the foundation of modern LLMs.|GPT, Claude, Gemini and BERT are all built on the transformer architecture.
Attention Mechanism|Attention lets a model weigh how relevant every other part of the input is when processing each part. This lets it use context, for example working out what "it" refers to in a sentence.|In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired", attention links "it" to "animal".
Self-attention|In self-attention, each word in a sequence attends to all the other words in the same sequence to build a context-aware representation of itself. It is the core operation of the transformer.|The word "bank" in "river bank" becomes a different internal representation from "bank" in "bank account".
Multi-head Attention|Multi-head attention runs several attention operations in parallel, each free to focus on a different kind of relationship, such as grammar, meaning or references. Their results are then combined.|One head tracks which noun a pronoun refers to; another tracks which verb goes with which subject.
Encoder|An encoder reads the input and turns it into a rich internal representation that captures its meaning. Encoder-only models are good at understanding tasks like classification and search.|BERT is an encoder model used to understand search queries.
Decoder|A decoder generates output one token at a time from an internal representation and what it has produced so far. Decoder-only models, like most chatbots, are good at generating text.|GPT models are decoders that write text one token after another.
Encoder-Decoder|An encoder-decoder model first encodes the whole input, then decodes an output sequence from it. It suits tasks that transform one sequence into another.|A translation model encodes an English sentence and decodes it into German.
Positional Encoding|Because attention looks at all words at once, it has no built-in sense of word order. Positional encodings add information about each token's position so the model knows "dog bites man" from "man bites dog".|Adding a unique position signal to each word's embedding before the first transformer layer.
Autoencoder|An autoencoder learns to compress its input into a small code and then rebuild the original from that code. To do this it must learn the most important features. It is used for compression, denoising and anomaly detection.|An autoencoder trained on normal machine sounds fails to rebuild a faulty sound well, flagging a problem.
Variational Autoencoder (VAE)|A VAE is an autoencoder whose compressed codes follow a smooth, structured distribution, so you can sample new codes and decode them into brand-new, realistic data.|Generating new face images by sampling random points in a VAE's latent space.
GAN (Generative Adversarial Network)|A GAN trains two networks against each other: a generator makes fake data, and a discriminator tries to tell fakes from real data. Both improve until the fakes look convincing. GANs produced the first very realistic AI faces.|The website "This Person Does Not Exist" showed GAN-generated faces of people who don't exist.
Generator|The generator is the creative half of a GAN. It turns random noise into data, such as images, and learns from the discriminator's feedback how to make its output more realistic.|A generator that starts by producing blurry noise and ends up producing sharp photos of bedrooms.
Discriminator|The discriminator is the critic in a GAN. It learns to tell real data from the generator's fakes, and its judgements are the training signal that pushes the generator to improve.|A discriminator that learns to spot tiny artefacts in fake faces, forcing the generator to remove them.
Diffusion Model|A diffusion model learns to reverse a process that gradually adds noise to images. To generate, it starts from pure noise and removes it step by step, guided by a text prompt, until a clear image appears.|Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 3 and Midjourney are built on diffusion models.
Residual Connection (Skip Connection)|A residual connection adds a layer's input directly to its output, giving information and gradients a shortcut around the layer. This makes very deep networks much easier to train.|ResNet used skip connections to train networks with over 100 layers successfully.
ResNet|ResNet (2015) is a deep image-recognition network built from residual blocks. It showed that very deep networks could be trained reliably and won major image-recognition competitions.|ResNet-50, with 50 layers, is still a common starting point for image tasks.
Dropout|Dropout randomly switches off a fraction of neurons during each training step, so the network can't rely too heavily on any single neuron. This reduces overfitting. At prediction time all neurons are used.|Dropping 20% of neurons at random in every training step of a text classifier.
Batch Normalization|Batch normalization rescales each layer's inputs within every batch so they have a consistent average and spread. This stabilises and speeds up training of deep networks.|Adding batch normalization after each convolution so a deep image network trains several times faster.
Layer Normalization|Layer normalization rescales values across the features of each individual example rather than across a batch. It works well for sequences and is used in every transformer layer.|Each token's representation in a transformer is normalised before and after its attention step.
Embedding Layer|An embedding layer turns discrete items, like words or product IDs, into lists of numbers (vectors) that the model can work with. These vectors are learned, so similar items end up with similar vectors.|A movie recommender learns vectors in which similar films sit close together.
Latent Space|The latent space is the hidden space of internal representations a model learns. Points that are close together represent similar things, and moving through it changes the output smoothly.|In an image generator, moving through latent space can gradually turn a smiling face into a frowning one.
Mixture of Experts (MoE)|A mixture-of-experts model contains many sub-networks (experts) and a router that sends each token to only a few of them. This gives a model huge capacity while keeping the compute per token low.|A model with 64 experts might activate only 2 for each token, using a fraction of its total parameters at a time.
Graph Neural Network (GNN)|A GNN works on data structured as a graph of nodes and connections. Each node updates its representation using information from its neighbours, so the model learns from relationships.|Predicting drug properties from the graph of atoms and bonds in a molecule.
Capsule Network|Capsule networks, proposed by Geoffrey Hinton, group neurons into capsules that represent both whether an object is present and its pose. They aim to understand spatial relationships better than CNNs, but remain mostly experimental.|A capsule network trying to recognise a face even when it is upside down.
Spiking Neural Network|Spiking neural networks mimic biological neurons more closely: neurons send brief spikes at particular moments rather than continuous values. They can be very energy-efficient on special "neuromorphic" chips.|Intel's Loihi chip runs spiking networks for low-power sensing in robots.
Architecture|A model's architecture is its overall design: what kinds of layers it uses, how many, and how they connect. The architecture sets what a model can learn; training fills in the details.|CNNs, RNNs and transformers are different architectures suited to different kinds of data.
Linear Regression|Linear regression fits the straight line, or flat surface, that best predicts a number from one or more features, by minimising squared errors. It is simple, fast and easy to explain, and often a strong baseline.|Predicting a house's price as a base amount plus a fixed amount per square metre.
Logistic Regression|Despite the name, logistic regression is a classification method. It combines features linearly, then uses a sigmoid to output a probability between 0 and 1. It is fast, reliable and easy to interpret.|Estimating the probability that a customer will click on an ad.
Polynomial Regression|Polynomial regression fits a curve by including powers of the features, such as x squared. It can capture bends in the data, but high powers easily overfit, as the Module 3 overfitting lab shows.|Fitting a curve to how plant growth rises and then levels off with fertiliser amount.
Decision Tree|A decision tree asks a series of yes/no questions about the features, branching at each answer, until it reaches a prediction. It is easy to understand and explain, but a single tree overfits easily.|"Is income above $40k? Is the debt ratio below 30%?" leading to "approve loan".
Random Forest|A random forest trains many decision trees, each on a random sample of the data and features, then averages or votes on their predictions. It is accurate, robust and works well with little tuning.|A random forest predicting which hospital patients are at high risk of readmission.
Gradient Boosting|Gradient boosting builds trees one after another, with each new tree trained to fix the errors the previous ones made. It is often the most accurate method for table-style data.|Gradient-boosted trees are a favourite for winning data-science competitions on spreadsheet data.
XGBoost|XGBoost is a fast, highly optimised library for gradient boosting, with built-in regularisation and handling of missing values. It became famous for winning many machine-learning competitions.|A bank uses XGBoost to score credit applications from hundreds of features.
LightGBM / CatBoost|LightGBM (from Microsoft) and CatBoost (from Yandex) are popular gradient-boosting libraries. LightGBM is known for speed on large datasets; CatBoost is known for handling categorical features well.|Using CatBoost on customer data full of categories like city, device type and plan.
AdaBoost|AdaBoost was one of the first boosting methods. It trains simple models in sequence, giving more weight each round to the examples that earlier models got wrong.|An early face detector in cameras used AdaBoost to find faces quickly.
Bagging|Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains many models on different random samples of the data, drawn with replacement, and averages their predictions. This reduces variance and overfitting.|Random forests are bagging applied to decision trees.
Boosting|Boosting combines many weak models in sequence, each focusing on the mistakes of the ones before, to build one strong model. It mainly reduces bias.|Gradient boosting and AdaBoost are both boosting methods.
Stacking|Stacking trains several different models, then trains a final "meta-model" that learns how best to combine their predictions. It can squeeze out extra accuracy.|Combining a random forest, a neural network and logistic regression with a meta-model on top.
Support Vector Machine (SVM)|An SVM finds the boundary between classes that leaves the widest possible margin to the nearest examples. With "kernel" tricks it can also draw curved boundaries. It was very popular before deep learning.|Classifying handwritten digits or detecting faces in early-2000s systems.
K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)|KNN predicts by finding the k most similar examples in the training data and taking their majority label or average value. There is no training step, but predictions can be slow on large data.|Recommending a film by looking at the 5 users most similar to you and what they liked.
Naive Bayes|Naive Bayes uses Bayes' theorem to estimate how likely each class is from the features, "naively" assuming each feature is independent. It is fast, needs little data and works well for text.|The spam filter in Module 3 is a Naive Bayes classifier counting words in spam and normal emails.
K-Means Clustering|K-means splits data into k groups by repeatedly assigning each point to the nearest group centre, then moving each centre to the middle of its points. You must choose k yourself.|Grouping 10,000 customers into 4 segments by spending and visit frequency.
Hierarchical Clustering|Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of clusters by repeatedly merging the closest groups, or splitting the largest. You can cut the tree at any level to get more or fewer groups.|Building a family tree of animal species from the similarity of their DNA.
DBSCAN|DBSCAN finds clusters as dense regions of points and labels points in sparse areas as noise. It can find clusters of any shape and doesn't need the number of clusters in advance.|Finding hotspots of taxi pickups on a city map while ignoring scattered one-off rides.
Principal Component Analysis (PCA)|PCA finds new axes, called principal components, that capture the most variation in the data, and keeps only the top few. It reduces dimensions while losing as little information as possible.|Reducing 50 correlated health measurements to 3 components that explain most of the differences between patients.
t-SNE / UMAP|t-SNE and UMAP squash high-dimensional data into 2D or 3D for plotting, keeping similar points close together. They are excellent for exploring data visually, but distances between far-apart clusters can be misleading.|Plotting thousands of word embeddings so that related words appear in visible clusters.
Apriori|Apriori finds sets of items that frequently appear together, then turns them into association rules. It saves time by ignoring any larger set that contains an infrequent smaller set.|Mining supermarket receipts to find "bread and butter are bought together 30% of the time".
Collaborative Filtering|Collaborative filtering recommends items based on the behaviour of similar users, without needing to know anything about the items themselves. It struggles with brand-new users and items, the "cold start" problem.|"People who liked the same films as you also liked this one."
Content-based Filtering|Content-based filtering recommends items that are similar to ones a user already liked, based on the items' features such as genre, author or keywords.|Recommending another crime thriller because you finished three crime thrillers.
Markov Chain|A Markov chain models a sequence of states where the next state depends only on the current one, with fixed probabilities. It is simple but powerful for modelling sequences.|A weather model where tomorrow's chance of rain depends only on whether it rained today.
Hidden Markov Model (HMM)|An HMM is a Markov chain whose states can't be seen directly, only inferred from observations they produce. HMMs were central to speech recognition and gene analysis before deep learning.|Inferring which words were spoken (hidden) from the sound waves recorded (observed).
Genetic Algorithm|A genetic algorithm evolves a population of candidate solutions: the fittest are selected, combined and slightly mutated to form the next generation. It is useful for complex problems without a clear formula.|Evolving antenna shapes; NASA used this approach to design an antenna for a spacecraft.
Evolutionary Algorithm|Evolutionary algorithms are a family of optimisation methods inspired by natural evolution, using selection, variation and inheritance. Genetic algorithms are one type.|Evolving the settings of a neural network instead of tuning them by hand.
Search Algorithm|A search algorithm systematically explores possible states or solutions to find a goal or the best option. Classic AI was largely built on clever search, and it still matters in planning and games.|Exploring possible moves in a puzzle until a solution is found.
A* Search|A* search finds the shortest path by combining the distance travelled so far with a heuristic estimate of the distance remaining. It is efficient and guaranteed to find the best path if the heuristic never overestimates.|Video game characters and navigation apps use A*-style search to find routes.
Minimax|Minimax chooses moves in two-player games by assuming the opponent always plays their best response, so it picks the move with the best worst-case outcome. It is usually combined with pruning to skip hopeless branches.|Classic chess and tic-tac-toe programs use minimax to look several moves ahead.
Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)|MCTS decides moves by running many random simulated games from the current position and focusing on the moves that win most often. Combined with neural networks it beat world champions at Go.|AlphaGo used MCTS guided by neural networks to choose its moves.
Bayesian Network|A Bayesian network is a graph of variables linked by probabilistic cause-and-effect relationships. It can reason under uncertainty and update its beliefs when new evidence arrives.|A medical network linking diseases to symptoms, estimating how likely flu is given fever and cough.
ARIMA|ARIMA is a classic statistical model for time series. It combines past values, past errors and differencing to remove trends. It remains a strong, explainable baseline for forecasting.|Forecasting monthly airline passenger numbers from previous years.
Accuracy|Accuracy is the share of all predictions that were correct. It is easy to understand, but misleading when one class is rare: a model that always predicts the common class can still score very highly.|A model that gets 90 out of 100 test emails right has 90% accuracy.
Precision|Precision answers "when the model says yes, how often is it right?" High precision means few false alarms. It matters most when false alarms are costly.|If a spam filter flags 100 emails and 95 really are spam, its precision is 95%.
Recall (Sensitivity)|Recall answers "of all the real yes cases, how many did the model find?" High recall means few missed cases. It matters most when missing a case is dangerous.|If 100 patients have a disease and the test catches 92 of them, its recall is 92%.
F1 Score|The F1 score combines precision and recall into one number, and is only high when both are high. It is useful when you care about both kinds of error and the classes are unbalanced.|A model with 80% precision and 60% recall has an F1 score of about 69%.
Specificity|Specificity answers "of all the real no cases, how many did the model correctly leave alone?" It is the counterpart to recall for the negative class.|If 900 healthy patients are tested and 855 are correctly told they are healthy, specificity is 95%.
Confusion Matrix|A confusion matrix is a table that counts correct and incorrect predictions for each class: true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives. It shows exactly what kinds of mistakes a model makes.|The Module 3 spam lab shows a confusion matrix of caught spam, missed spam, false alarms and correct normal emails.
True Positive / True Negative|A true positive is a correct "yes" and a true negative is a correct "no". Together they make up the model's correct predictions.|A spam email correctly flagged is a true positive; a normal email correctly delivered is a true negative.
False Positive (Type I Error)|A false positive is a false alarm: the model says yes when the true answer is no. Too many false positives annoy users and waste time.|An important work email wrongly sent to the spam folder.
False Negative (Type II Error)|A false negative is a miss: the model says no when the true answer is yes. In medicine or security, misses can be the most dangerous error.|A screening test that tells a sick patient they are healthy.
ROC Curve|A ROC curve plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate as the decision threshold moves from strict to lenient. It shows the trade-off between catching cases and raising false alarms.|Plotting how a fraud model catches more fraud, but also flags more genuine payments, as the threshold is lowered.
AUC (Area Under the Curve)|AUC summarises the ROC curve as one number between 0.5 (random guessing) and 1.0 (perfect ranking). It measures how well a model ranks positives above negatives, whatever threshold you choose.|A credit model with an AUC of 0.85 ranks a random defaulter above a random non-defaulter 85% of the time.
Mean Squared Error (MSE)|MSE averages the squared differences between predictions and true values. Squaring punishes large errors heavily, so the measure is sensitive to big misses.|Predicting prices of $100k and $200k for houses really worth $110k and $180k gives an MSE of 250 million (in dollars squared).
RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error)|RMSE is the square root of MSE, which puts the error back into the original units. It is easier to interpret while still punishing big errors more.|An RMSE of $15,000 means predictions are typically about $15,000 off, with big misses weighted more.
Mean Absolute Error (MAE)|MAE averages the absolute size of the errors. It is easy to explain and less sensitive to occasional huge errors than MSE.|A delivery-time model with an MAE of 6 minutes is off by 6 minutes on average.
R-squared|R-squared shows what share of the variation in the target the model explains, from 0 (nothing) to 1 (everything). It is useful for regression, but a high value doesn't prove the model is right.|An R-squared of 0.8 means the model explains 80% of the variation in house prices.
Log Loss (Cross-entropy)|Log loss judges predicted probabilities, not just right or wrong answers. It heavily punishes confident wrong predictions. It is also the loss used to train most classifiers and language models.|Predicting 99% spam for an email that isn't spam costs far more log loss than predicting 60%.
Perplexity|Perplexity measures how surprised a language model is by real text; lower means it predicts the text better. A perplexity of 10 is like choosing between 10 equally likely next words at each step.|A better model has lower perplexity on a held-out set of news articles.
BLEU Score|BLEU compares a machine translation with human reference translations by counting matching word sequences. It is quick and automatic, but it misses meaning, so human judgement still matters.|A translation sharing many phrases with the human version gets a higher BLEU score.
ROUGE Score|ROUGE compares an automatic summary with human-written summaries by measuring overlapping words and phrases, focusing on how much of the reference content was covered.|Scoring an AI summary of a news article against an editor's summary.
Benchmark|A benchmark is a standard dataset and task used to compare models fairly. Benchmarks drive progress, but models can become tuned to them and still fail on real tasks.|ImageNet for image recognition and MMLU for broad knowledge are well-known benchmarks.
MMLU|MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) tests language models with multiple-choice questions across 57 subjects, from law to physics. Top models now score so highly that harder benchmarks are needed.|A model answering high-school and professional exam questions in history, medicine and maths.
Evals|Evals are tests designed to measure how well an AI system does a specific job, including quality, safety and reliability. Good evals use realistic inputs and clear criteria, and are re-run whenever the system changes.|A company tests its support chatbot on 200 real customer questions, scoring accuracy and politeness.
Baseline|A baseline is a simple reference model that a new model must beat to be worth it. Without a baseline you can't tell whether a fancy model is actually adding value.|Before building a neural network, checking how well "predict last week's sales again" performs.
A/B Testing|A/B testing compares two versions with real users by randomly showing each group a different one and measuring outcomes. It shows real-world impact, not just test-set scores.|Half of users see the old recommendation model and half see the new one; the new one gets 4% more clicks.
Human Evaluation|In human evaluation, people judge the quality of outputs where automatic measures fall short, such as helpfulness, tone or creativity. It is slower and more expensive, but often the most trustworthy check.|Raters compare two chatbot answers side by side and pick the more helpful one.
LLM-as-a-Judge|LLM-as-a-judge uses a strong language model to grade other models' outputs against criteria. It is fast and cheap, but it can be biased, for example towards longer answers, so it should be checked against human ratings.|Using one AI to score 1,000 chatbot answers for accuracy and clarity overnight.
Leaderboard|A leaderboard ranks models by their benchmark scores. It is useful for a quick comparison, but small differences may not matter, and leaderboard scores don't always reflect real-world usefulness.|Public leaderboards that rank chatbots by thousands of human head-to-head votes.
Error Analysis|Error analysis means reading through a model's mistakes to find patterns, such as certain groups, topics or input types it fails on. It often reveals the most effective next fix.|Discovering that a speech model fails mostly on children's voices, then collecting more of them.
Statistics|Statistics is the science of collecting, summarising and drawing conclusions from data while accounting for uncertainty. Much of machine learning is built on statistical ideas.|Estimating the average height of a country's adults from a sample of 1,000 people.
Probability|Probability measures how likely an event is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). AI models usually output probabilities rather than certainties, which is why they can be confidently wrong.|A model saying there is a 0.8 probability that a photo shows a cat.
Probability Distribution|A probability distribution describes how likely each possible value is. Language models produce a distribution over all possible next tokens, then sample one from it.|After "Once upon a", the distribution puts about 94% on "time" and spreads the rest over other words.
Normal Distribution|The normal distribution is the familiar symmetric bell curve where most values cluster around the average. Many natural measurements and measurement errors roughly follow it.|Adult heights in a population roughly follow a normal distribution.
Mean, Median, Mode|The mean is the average, the median is the middle value, and the mode is the most common value. When data is skewed by extreme values, the median often describes it better than the mean.|In incomes of 20, 25, 30 and 1,000, the mean is 268.75 but the median is 27.5.
Variance|Variance measures how spread out values are around the mean, by averaging the squared distances from it. High variance means the values vary a lot.|Daily sales of 100, 101 and 99 have low variance; 20, 200 and 80 have high variance.
Standard Deviation|Standard deviation is the square root of variance, in the same units as the data. Roughly two-thirds of normally distributed values lie within one standard deviation of the mean.|Exam scores with a mean of 70 and a standard deviation of 10 mean most students scored between 60 and 80.
Correlation|Correlation measures how strongly two variables move together, from −1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (together). It says nothing about whether one causes the other.|Ice cream sales and sunburn cases are positively correlated, because both rise in hot weather.
Causation|Causation means one thing actually produces a change in another. Proving it usually needs controlled experiments, not just observed correlations, and models that confuse the two can mislead decisions.|Smoking causes lung cancer, which was established through large studies, not just correlation.
Bayes' Theorem|Bayes' theorem shows how to update the probability of a belief when new evidence arrives, combining your prior belief with how likely the evidence is under each possibility.|Updating the chance a patient has a rare disease after a positive test, taking into account how rare the disease is.
Linear Algebra|Linear algebra is the maths of vectors and matrices. Neural networks are, at heart, huge chains of matrix multiplications, which is why GPUs, built for this maths, are so important.|Passing data through a network layer means multiplying the input vector by a weight matrix.
Vector|A vector is an ordered list of numbers, such as a point's coordinates or a word's embedding. In AI, vectors represent data in a form that can be compared and computed with.|A word embedding might be a vector of 768 numbers.
Matrix|A matrix is a rectangular grid of numbers. In AI, a matrix often holds a layer's weights, or a batch of data with one row per example.|A greyscale image is a matrix of pixel brightness values.
Tensor|A tensor is a multi-dimensional array of numbers: a vector is 1-D and a matrix is 2-D, while tensors can have more dimensions. Deep learning libraries store all data and weights as tensors.|A batch of 32 colour images of 224 by 224 pixels is a 4-D tensor of shape 32 × 224 × 224 × 3.
Dot Product|The dot product multiplies two vectors element by element and adds up the results. It measures how aligned they are, and it is the basic operation behind neurons and attention.|The dot product of (1, 2, 3) and (4, 5, 6) is 4 + 10 + 18 = 32.
Calculus|Calculus is the maths of change. In AI it is used to compute gradients, which tell training how to adjust each weight to reduce error.|Calculating how the error would change if one weight were increased slightly.
Derivative|A derivative measures how fast a function's output changes as its input changes, which is the slope at a point. Gradients are collections of derivatives, one for each weight.|The derivative of position with respect to time is speed.
Optimization|Optimization means finding the inputs or settings that give the best value of some objective, such as the lowest error. Training a model is an optimization problem.|Adjusting millions of weights to minimise a model's prediction errors.
Cosine Similarity|Cosine similarity measures how similar two vectors are by the angle between them, ignoring their length. It ranges from −1 to 1 and is widely used to compare embeddings in search.|"King" and "queen" have embeddings with high cosine similarity; "king" and "banana" do not.
Euclidean Distance|Euclidean distance is the straight-line distance between two points, calculated with Pythagoras' theorem. It is used in clustering and nearest-neighbour methods.|The distance between points (0, 0) and (3, 4) is 5.
Hypothesis Testing|Hypothesis testing checks whether an observed effect is likely to be real or could easily be due to chance. It is the basis of A/B testing and scientific experiments.|Testing whether a new website design really increases sign-ups or the difference is just random luck.
P-value|A p-value is the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if there were really no effect. Small p-values, often below 0.05, suggest the effect is unlikely to be pure chance. It is often misunderstood.|A p-value of 0.01 means a difference this big would happen by chance only about 1% of the time if there were no real effect.
Entropy|Entropy measures uncertainty or unpredictability: it is high when outcomes are evenly spread and low when one outcome is almost certain. It underlies log loss and how decision trees choose splits.|A fair coin flip has high entropy; a coin that lands heads 99% of the time has low entropy.
Logit|A logit is a model's raw, unscaled score for a class or token before it is turned into a probability, for example by softmax. Temperature works by scaling logits.|A language model outputs logits for 100,000 possible tokens, then softmax turns them into probabilities.
Stochastic|Stochastic means involving randomness. Many AI processes are stochastic, such as sampling the next word or picking random batches, so the same input can give different outputs.|A chatbot giving slightly different answers to the same question is behaving stochastically.
Deterministic|Deterministic means the same input always gives exactly the same output. Rule-based systems are deterministic; language models can be made nearly deterministic with temperature 0.|A calculator is deterministic: 2 + 2 is always 4.
Prompt|A prompt is everything you give the model: the question, instructions, examples, context and any pasted text. The model has no other information about what you want, so the prompt shapes the answer more than anything else you control.|"Summarise this article in 3 bullet points for a busy manager" plus the article text.
Prompt Engineering|Prompt engineering is the skill of designing and refining prompts to get reliable, high-quality results. It includes clear instructions, examples, structure and testing, and it is iterative rather than one-shot.|Turning "write about dogs" into a precise prompt with an audience, length, format and example.
System Prompt|The system prompt is a set of background instructions that frames the whole conversation: the AI's role, rules, tone and limits. Users usually don't see it, but it shapes every reply.|A company's support bot has a system prompt saying "You are a polite assistant for Acme Bank. Never give investment advice."
User Prompt|The user prompt is the message the user types in each turn. It is combined with the system prompt and the conversation history before the model replies.|"What's the weather like in Paris in April?"
Zero-shot Prompting|Zero-shot prompting asks the model to do a task with instructions only and no examples. It works well for common tasks the model already understands.|"Classify this review as positive, negative or neutral: The food was cold."
Few-shot Prompting|Few-shot prompting includes a few worked examples in the prompt so the model copies the pattern, format or style. Varied, high-quality examples work best.|Giving three examples of product features turned into benefits, then asking for a fourth.
Chain-of-Thought (CoT)|Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to reason step by step before giving its answer. Writing out intermediate steps often improves accuracy on maths, logic and multi-step problems.|"A shop sells pens at 3 for $2. How much do 20 pens cost? Think step by step."
Tree of Thoughts|Tree of thoughts has the model explore several possible lines of reasoning, evaluate them, and continue the most promising, like exploring branches of a tree. It helps with puzzles and planning.|Asking the model to propose three approaches to a problem, rate each one, then develop the best.
Self-consistency|Self-consistency generates several independent answers or reasoning paths and takes the most common final answer. It reduces random mistakes on problems with one right answer.|Running a maths question five times and choosing the answer that appears most often.
Role Prompting|Role prompting asks the model to act as a particular expert or character. It shifts the tone, focus and vocabulary of the answer, but it doesn't give the model knowledge it lacks.|"You are a patient primary-school teacher. Explain photosynthesis."
Prompt Template|A prompt template is a reusable prompt with blanks to fill in. Templates keep results consistent across many uses and make prompts easy to share and improve.|"Write a {length} product description for {product} aimed at {audience}."
Prompt Chaining|Prompt chaining splits a big task into steps, feeding each step's output into the next prompt. It is easier to check, debug and improve than one giant prompt.|Step 1: outline the report. Step 2: write each section. Step 3: write the executive summary.
Meta-prompting|Meta-prompting uses an AI to write, critique or improve prompts. It can speed things up, but the improved prompt still needs testing on real inputs.|"Here is my prompt. Suggest three ways to make the answers more specific."
Context Engineering|Context engineering means deciding everything the model sees: instructions, examples, retrieved documents, tool results and conversation history, and how they are ordered. It matters most in complex AI applications.|A support assistant automatically includes the customer's last order and the relevant help article in its context.
Persona|A persona is a character, voice or identity the AI is asked to adopt consistently. It is useful for practice partners, branded assistants and storytelling.|"You are Captain Nova, a cheerful space explorer who explains science to children."
Output Format|Specifying the output format tells the model exactly how to structure its answer, such as a table, a numbered list, JSON or a word limit. Clear formats make answers easier to use and check.|"Answer as a table with columns: Step, What to do, Time needed."
Structured Output|Structured output is a strict, machine-readable format, such as JSON following a schema, so software can use the answer directly. Many tools can enforce the schema.|A model returning {"name": "Ana", "age": 34} so an app can store it in a database.
Delimiter|Delimiters, such as triple quotes, XML tags or ###, mark where pasted text starts and ends. They help the model separate your instructions from the data, and reduce prompt injection risks.|"Summarise the text between the triple quotes: """…article…""""
Prefill|Prefilling means writing the first part of the model's reply yourself, so it continues in the direction you want. It is useful for forcing a format or a starting phrase.|Starting the reply with "{" to make the model answer in JSON.
Iterative Prompting|Iterative prompting improves results through several rounds of feedback and refinement, rather than expecting a perfect first answer.|"Shorter." "Add an example." "Make the tone friendlier." until the draft is right.
Custom Instructions|Custom instructions are saved preferences an AI tool applies to all your chats, such as your role, preferred tone or format. They save repeating yourself.|"I'm a teacher. Keep explanations simple and always include a classroom example."
Large Language Model (LLM)|An LLM is a very large neural network, usually a transformer, trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next token. That simple skill, at scale, lets it write, summarise, translate, answer questions and write code. It produces likely text, not checked facts.|ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Llama are all large language models.
Small Language Model (SLM)|A small language model has far fewer parameters, from millions to a few billion, so it is cheaper and faster and can run on a phone or laptop without the internet. It is less capable at complex reasoning but good for focused tasks.|A phone's on-device model that summarises notifications without sending them to the cloud.
Language Model|A language model assigns probabilities to sequences of words and predicts what comes next. Early ones counted word sequences; modern ones are neural networks. They power autocomplete, speech recognition and chatbots.|Your keyboard suggesting "you" after you type "thank".
Natural Language Processing (NLP)|NLP is the field of making computers understand, process and generate human language. It covers translation, sentiment analysis, search, chatbots and more, and large language models have transformed it.|An email app sorting incoming mail into "promotions", "social" and "primary".
Natural Language Understanding (NLU)|NLU focuses on working out what text means: the intent, the entities and the sentiment behind the words. It is the "reading" half of NLP.|A voice assistant recognising that "wake me at 7" means "set an alarm for 7:00".
Natural Language Generation (NLG)|NLG focuses on producing human-like text from data or prompts. It ranges from simple templates to fluent LLM writing.|Automatically writing a weather report from forecast data.
Foundation Model|A foundation model is a large model trained on broad data that serves as a base for many different applications through prompting or fine-tuning. Building one is extremely expensive; using one is not.|Thousands of apps are built on top of a few foundation models like GPT, Claude and Gemini.
Frontier Model|A frontier model is one of the most capable AI models available at a given time, typically from a leading lab. These models get the most attention for both their abilities and their risks.|Each new top model release from OpenAI, Anthropic or Google DeepMind is a frontier model when it launches.
GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer)|GPT names both an architecture idea and OpenAI's model family: a transformer, pre-trained on huge text, that generates text. GPT models popularised the modern LLM approach.|GPT-3 (2020) showed that a single large model could do many tasks just from prompts.
BERT|BERT (2018, Google) is an encoder transformer that reads text in both directions to understand it deeply. It isn't built to chat, but it greatly improved search and text classification.|Google Search used BERT to better understand the meaning of queries.
Token|A token is the unit of text an LLM reads and writes: a whole common word, part of a rare word, a number or a punctuation mark. Prices, limits and context windows are all counted in tokens.|"Tokenization" might be split into "Token" and "ization", which is 2 tokens.
Tokenization|Tokenization splits text into tokens and converts each one into a number ID before the model sees it. How text is tokenized affects cost, multilingual fairness and odd behaviours like miscounting letters.|The Module 4 tokenizer lab shows any sentence split into real GPT-4 tokens.
Tokenizer|The tokenizer is the tool that converts text to token IDs and back again. Each model family has its own tokenizer and vocabulary, so the same text can be a different number of tokens in different models.|GPT-4's tokenizer turns "I love strawberries" into 3 tokens.
Vocabulary (model)|A model's vocabulary is the fixed list of all tokens it knows, typically 30,000 to 200,000. Any text must be expressed using these tokens.|A vocabulary might contain "the", " cat" and "ization", but rare words must be built from smaller pieces.
Context Window|The context window is the maximum amount of text, measured in tokens, that a model can consider at once: the system prompt, the conversation, any documents and its own reply. Anything outside it is invisible to the model.|A model with a 128,000-token window can read roughly a 300-page book at once.
Context Length|Context length is the size of the context window in tokens. Longer context lets the model handle bigger documents, but it costs more and the model may use details in the middle less well.|A context length of 8,000 tokens is about 6,000 words.
Next-token Prediction|Next-token prediction is the single task LLMs are trained on: given the text so far, predict the probability of every possible next token. Writing a whole answer means repeating this step thousands of times.|Given "The cat sat on the", the model gives "mat" the highest probability.
Autoregressive|Autoregressive generation produces output one piece at a time, with each new token predicted from everything before it, including the tokens the model just wrote. That's why early mistakes can snowball.|An LLM writing a story word by word, with each word depending on all the previous ones.
Inference|Inference is using a trained model to produce outputs, as opposed to training it. Every chatbot reply is inference, and at large scale inference costs can exceed training costs.|Each time you send a message to a chatbot, the provider runs inference on its servers.
Completion|A completion is the text a model generates in response to an input. The term comes from early models that literally completed a piece of text.|Given "Roses are red,", the completion might be " violets are blue."
Input Tokens / Output Tokens|Input tokens are what you send to the model (prompt, history, documents); output tokens are what it writes back. Providers usually charge for both, with output tokens often costing more.|A request with 2,000 input tokens and 500 output tokens is billed for 2,500 tokens, at different rates.
Temperature|Temperature controls how random the next-token choice is. Low temperature almost always picks the most likely token, which is focused and repeatable; high temperature gives less likely tokens a bigger chance, which is more creative but riskier.|The Module 4 next-word game shows "Sydney" appearing more often as the temperature rises.
Top-k Sampling|Top-k sampling restricts the choice of next token to the k most likely options, then samples among them. It prevents very unlikely, odd tokens from being chosen.|With k = 5, only the five most likely next words can ever be picked.
Top-p (Nucleus) Sampling|Top-p sampling picks from the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, such as 0.9. The set grows when the model is unsure and shrinks when it is confident.|With p = 0.9 after "Once upon a", only "time" may be needed, since it alone covers 94%.
Max Tokens|Max tokens sets the upper limit on how long the model's reply can be. If the reply hits the limit, it stops mid-sentence.|Setting max tokens to 100 for short product descriptions.
Stop Sequence|A stop sequence is a string of text that tells the model to stop generating as soon as it produces it. It is used to keep outputs to the right shape.|Stopping generation at "\n\nUser:" so the model doesn't invent the user's next message.
Hallucination|A hallucination is when an AI confidently produces false or invented information, such as fake facts, quotes, citations or events. It happens because the model generates likely-sounding text rather than verified facts.|An AI inventing a realistic-looking court case that never existed, complete with case number.
Confabulation|Confabulation is another word for hallucination, borrowed from psychology, where it describes people filling memory gaps with invented details without meaning to lie. Some researchers prefer it as more accurate.|A chatbot making up a plausible biography for a little-known person.
Grounding|Grounding ties a model's answers to specific, trusted information, such as documents you paste, a database or search results, instead of relying only on its training. It is the main defence against hallucination.|"Answer using only the policy document below, and say if the answer isn't in it."
Knowledge Cutoff|The knowledge cutoff is the date after which the model's training data stops. Without tools like web search, it can't know about later events, and may not realise that it doesn't know.|A model with a 2024 cutoff can't tell you who won a 2025 election unless it searches.
Instruction Tuning|Instruction tuning fine-tunes a pre-trained model on many examples of instructions paired with good responses, turning a text-continuer into a model that follows requests.|After instruction tuning, "Summarise this" produces a summary instead of more text in the same style.
Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)|SFT trains a model on example prompts paired with ideal answers written or approved by people. It is usually the first step in turning a base model into an assistant.|Thousands of hand-written question-answer pairs are used to teach a model a helpful conversational style.
RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)|In RLHF, people compare pairs of model answers and pick the better one. A reward model learns their preferences, and the main model is then trained to produce answers that score well. It makes models more helpful and polite, but can also make them sycophantic.|Raters prefer answers that are clear and honest, so the model learns to answer that way.
RLAIF|RLAIF (reinforcement learning from AI feedback) replaces most human ratings with ratings from another AI, often guided by written principles. It is cheaper and faster to scale than human feedback.|An AI judge rates thousands of answers for harmlessness, and those ratings train the model.
Reward Model|A reward model is trained to predict how highly humans, or a set of principles, would rate an answer. It then acts as an automatic scorer during reinforcement learning.|A reward model gives a polite, accurate answer a high score and a rude one a low score.
DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)|DPO trains a model directly on pairs of preferred and rejected answers, without building a separate reward model. It is simpler and more stable than classic RLHF and is now widely used.|Training on 50,000 pairs where people marked which of two answers was better.
Constitutional AI|Constitutional AI, developed by Anthropic, trains a model to critique and revise its own responses against a written set of principles (a "constitution"), and uses AI feedback based on those principles. It reduces reliance on human labels for safety training.|A principle like "choose the response that is most helpful while avoiding harm" guides the model's self-revision.
Base Model|A base model is the raw result of pre-training. It knows a lot, but it simply continues text in the most likely way, so it may ignore instructions. It is the starting point for chat models.|Typing a question into a base model might produce more questions instead of an answer.
Chat Model|A chat model has been instruction-tuned and trained with feedback to hold helpful conversations, follow instructions and use a consistent assistant style. It is what most people use.|The models behind ChatGPT and Claude's chat apps are chat models.
Reasoning Model|A reasoning model is trained to think through problems step by step, often in a hidden "thinking" phase, before answering. It does much better on maths, coding and logic, at the cost of more time and tokens.|A reasoning model working through a hard maths problem for a minute before giving the answer.
Extended Thinking|Extended thinking gives a model extra time and token budget to reason before it replies. More thinking usually means better answers on complex problems, but slower responses.|Turning on extended thinking to have a model carefully plan a complex software change.
Test-time Compute|Test-time compute is the computation spent while answering rather than during training. Spending more of it, through longer reasoning or multiple attempts, can make a model significantly more accurate.|Letting a model try a coding problem 10 times and keep the version that passes the tests.
Multimodal|A multimodal model can take in or produce more than one kind of data, such as text, images, audio or video. This lets it describe photos, read charts or answer spoken questions.|Uploading a photo of a broken bike and asking the AI what part needs fixing.
Vision-Language Model (VLM)|A VLM understands images and text together, so it can describe pictures, answer questions about them or read text in them. Most modern assistants include vision abilities.|Asking an AI to explain the chart in a screenshot of a report.
Parameter Count|Parameter count is the number of learned weights in a model, often written like 7B or 70B (billions). More parameters usually means more capability, but also more memory, cost and energy.|A 7B model can run on a good laptop; a model with hundreds of billions of parameters needs a data centre.
Scaling Laws|Scaling laws are the observed, predictable relationships between a model's size, its training data, its compute, and how good it gets. They guide how labs plan and budget large training runs.|Doubling compute in a planned way gives a fairly predictable drop in error.
Emergent Abilities|Emergent abilities are skills that seem to appear only once models pass a certain size, such as multi-step arithmetic or following complex instructions. Researchers debate how sudden they really are.|Larger models suddenly succeed at tasks that smaller ones failed almost completely.
In-context Learning|In-context learning means a model picks up a new task from instructions or examples in the prompt, without any change to its weights. The "learning" lasts only for that conversation.|Showing three examples of a made-up code language and the model continues the pattern correctly.
LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)|LoRA fine-tunes a large model by training small add-on weight matrices instead of all the original weights. It needs a fraction of the memory and storage, and lets one base model have many small adapters.|Creating a 50-megabyte LoRA that teaches a model your company's writing style.
PEFT (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning)|PEFT is the family of methods, LoRA among them, that adapt big models by training only a small number of parameters. It makes fine-tuning affordable on modest hardware.|Fine-tuning a 7B model on a single gaming GPU using a PEFT method.
Quantization|Quantization stores a model's numbers with lower precision, for example 4 bits instead of 16. This makes the model much smaller and faster, usually with only a small loss in quality.|A quantized model that fits on a phone instead of needing a server.
Distillation|Distillation trains a smaller "student" model to imitate the outputs of a larger "teacher" model. The student keeps much of the ability at a fraction of the cost.|Many small, fast chatbot models are distilled from larger ones.
Pruning|Pruning removes weights or whole parts of a network that contribute little, making the model smaller and faster. It is often followed by brief retraining to recover accuracy.|Removing 40% of a network's connections with almost no drop in accuracy.
Open-weight Model|An open-weight model's trained weights are published for anyone to download and run, often with licence conditions. This allows private use and customisation, but also means misuse is harder to prevent.|Meta's Llama models and Mistral's models are open-weight.
Closed (Proprietary) Model|A closed model is available only through its company's apps or API; its weights stay private. The provider controls updates, safety measures and pricing.|GPT-4o, Claude and Gemini are used through their companies' services rather than downloaded.
Open Source AI|Open source AI shares a model's code, and often its weights and training details, so others can study, modify and build on it. There is ongoing debate about how "open" a model must be to count.|Researchers download an open model, study how it works and fine-tune it for a local language.
Sentiment Analysis|Sentiment analysis detects the emotional tone of text, such as positive, negative or neutral, and sometimes specific emotions. Businesses use it to track customer opinions at scale.|Analysing 10,000 product reviews to find which features customers complain about most.
Named Entity Recognition (NER)|NER finds and labels names of real-world things in text, such as people, organisations, places, dates and amounts. It is a key step in extracting structured information from documents.|In "Ana joined Google in Paris in 2021", NER tags Ana (person), Google (organisation), Paris (place) and 2021 (date).
Machine Translation|Machine translation automatically converts text from one language to another. Neural models made it dramatically better, though idioms, rare languages and specialist terms are still challenges.|Translating a restaurant menu with a phone camera app.
Summarization|Summarization produces a shorter version of a text that keeps its key points. Extractive summaries copy important sentences; abstractive summaries rewrite the ideas in new words.|Turning a 20-page report into a one-paragraph executive summary.
Text Classification|Text classification assigns categories to pieces of text, such as topic, intent, urgency or language. It is one of the most common practical NLP tasks.|Routing customer emails to billing, technical support or sales automatically.
Question Answering|Question answering systems answer questions in natural language, either from a given document or from general knowledge. Modern versions often combine retrieval with an LLM.|Asking "What is the refund period?" and getting the answer from a company's policy document.
Text-to-Image|Text-to-image models generate pictures from written descriptions. They learn the link between words and visual features from millions of image-caption pairs.|Typing "a red fox reading a book under a tree, watercolour" and getting a new illustration.
Text-to-Video|Text-to-video models generate short video clips from text descriptions, keeping objects and motion consistent from frame to frame. They are improving rapidly but are expensive to run.|Generating a 10-second clip of "waves crashing on a beach at sunset".
Text-to-Code|Text-to-code models turn plain-language descriptions into working program code. They speed up programming, but their code still needs testing and review.|"Write a Python function that checks whether an email address is valid."
Stable Diffusion|Stable Diffusion is a widely used open image-generation model released in 2022. Because its weights are public, it runs on personal computers and has been extended by a large community.|Artists running Stable Diffusion locally to generate concept art.
Latent Diffusion|Latent diffusion runs the diffusion process in a compressed "latent" version of the image instead of on full-size pixels. This makes image generation far faster and cheaper.|Stable Diffusion is a latent diffusion model, which is why it can run on consumer graphics cards.
Image Generation|Image generation means creating new images with AI, usually guided by text, sketches or other images. It is used in design, advertising and art, and raises copyright and misuse questions.|Generating product mock-ups for an online shop in minutes.
Inpainting|Inpainting fills in or replaces a selected area of an image so that it blends naturally with the rest. It is used for editing and repairing photos.|Removing a stranger from the background of a holiday photo.
Outpainting|Outpainting extends an image beyond its original borders, generating new content that continues the scene naturally.|Widening a portrait photo into a landscape by generating more of the background.
Upscaling|AI upscaling increases an image's resolution by predicting realistic fine detail, rather than just stretching the pixels.|Turning a small, blurry old photo into a sharp, large print.
Style Transfer|Style transfer re-renders one image in the visual style of another while keeping its content.|Turning a holiday photo into a painting in the style of Van Gogh's Starry Night.
Seed|A seed is the number that starts the random process in generation. Using the same seed with the same settings reproduces the same output, which helps with testing and fine-tuning results.|Re-using seed 42 to regenerate exactly the same image after tweaking one word.
Guidance Scale (CFG)|The guidance scale controls how strictly an image generator follows the prompt. Higher values follow the text more closely but can look less natural; lower values are more varied.|Raising the guidance scale when the generator keeps ignoring "wearing a red hat".

Deep Blue|Deep Blue was IBM's chess computer that beat world champion Garry Kasparov in a match in 1997. It relied on massive search and hand-tuned evaluation rather than learning.|The 1997 match was a landmark moment for AI in public awareness.
IBM Watson|IBM Watson won the TV quiz show Jeopardy! against top human champions in 2011 by combining language processing, search and evidence scoring. Later efforts to apply it to medicine struggled.|Watson answering Jeopardy! clues faster than the human champions.
ImageNet|ImageNet is a dataset of over 14 million labelled images in thousands of categories, created by Fei-Fei Li's team. Its yearly competition drove rapid progress and sparked the deep-learning boom.|Error rates in the ImageNet challenge fell dramatically after deep learning arrived in 2012.
AlexNet|AlexNet was the deep convolutional network that won the 2012 ImageNet competition by a huge margin, trained on GPUs. It convinced the field that deep learning works.|AlexNet's error rate was about 10 percentage points lower than the runner-up's.
AlphaGo|AlphaGo, from DeepMind, beat world Go champion Lee Sedol 4–1 in 2016, combining neural networks with tree search. Go had long been considered far too complex for computers.|AlphaGo's surprising "move 37" in game 2 astonished professional players.
AlphaFold|AlphaFold, from DeepMind, predicts the 3D structure of proteins from their amino-acid sequence with high accuracy, solving a 50-year-old biology challenge. Its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.|Scientists using AlphaFold's database of over 200 million predicted protein structures in drug research.
"Attention Is All You Need"|"Attention Is All You Need" is the 2017 Google research paper that introduced the transformer architecture. Almost every modern large language model descends from it.|GPT, BERT, Claude and Gemini all build on the architecture from this paper.
ChatGPT Launch (2022)|OpenAI released ChatGPT on 30 November 2022 as a free research preview. It reached about 100 million users within two months and triggered a global race in generative AI.|Within months, schools, businesses and governments were all debating how to respond to ChatGPT.
Godfathers of AI|Geoffrey Hinton, Yann LeCun and Yoshua Bengio are often called the godfathers of AI for their pioneering work on deep learning. They shared the 2018 Turing Award, and Hinton later shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics.|Hinton's work on backpropagation and neural networks underpins today's AI.
Moore's Law|Moore's Law is Gordon Moore's 1965 observation that the number of transistors on a chip doubled roughly every two years. This steady growth in computing power made modern AI possible, though the trend has slowed.|A phone today has far more computing power than the computers that guided the Apollo moon landings.
Singularity|The singularity is a hypothetical future point when AI becomes able to improve itself so rapidly that technological change becomes uncontrollable and unpredictable. It is a speculative and much-debated idea.|Futurist Ray Kurzweil has predicted the singularity around 2045.
Chinese Room|The Chinese Room is philosopher John Searle's 1980 thought experiment: a person following rules to answer Chinese messages, without understanding Chinese, appears fluent. He argued that manipulating symbols isn't the same as understanding.|Debating whether a chatbot that answers perfectly actually understands anything.
Three Laws of Robotics|Isaac Asimov's fictional Three Laws state that a robot must not harm humans, must obey humans, and must protect itself, in that order of priority. His stories show how simple rules can fail in surprising ways.|Asimov's "I, Robot" stories explore loopholes and conflicts between the laws.
OpenAI / Anthropic / Google DeepMind|These are three of the leading labs building frontier AI models: OpenAI (ChatGPT, GPT models), Anthropic (Claude) and Google DeepMind (Gemini, AlphaFold). They compete on capability and differ in approach to safety and openness.|Each lab's major model releases are widely compared on benchmarks and safety reports.
